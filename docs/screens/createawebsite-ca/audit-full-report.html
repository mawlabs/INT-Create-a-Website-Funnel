Safety and trust

Whether the connection can be trusted, and whether the site leaves easy openings for an attacker.

Worth fixing

  • The old insecure address still works

    Someone typing your address without “https” stays on the insecure version, and so do old links pointing at it.

    What to doAdd a permanent (301) redirect from http:// to https:// for the whole site.

Small things

  • The browser isn't told to always use the secure address

    A single header (HSTS) tells browsers never to try the insecure version again, which closes a small window during the first visit.

    What to doAdd the Strict-Transport-Security header once the https version is known to work everywhere.

  • The old WordPress remote interface is open

    xmlrpc.php lets software post to your site from outside. Almost nothing uses it any more, but password-guessing tools love it because it allows many tries in one request.

    What we sawhttps://boulangerie-exemple.ca/xmlrpc.php

    What to doBlock xmlrpc.php in your server config, or with a security plugin, unless you know something still needs it.

  • 3 standard protection headers are missing

    These are small server settings that block common attacks. They cost nothing and change nothing visible.

    What we sawx-content-type-options, referrer-policy, content-security-policy

    What to doAdd X-Content-Type-Options, Referrer-Policy and a Content-Security-Policy in your server config.

  • Your server announces its exact version

    The response says Apache/2.4.52 (Ubuntu). Attackers scan for exact versions with known weaknesses, so the fewer clues the better.

    What we sawApache/2.4.52 (Ubuntu)

    What to doTurn off the server signature (ServerTokens Prod in Apache, server_tokens off in nginx).

What it runs on

The versions behind the site. Out-of-date software is how most small sites get broken into.

Deal with these first

  • PHP 8.1.27 no longer gets security fixes

    PHP is the language your site runs on. Version 8.1.27 is past its end of life; 8.4 is current and considerably faster.

    What to doSwitch the PHP version in your hosting panel, after testing the site on a staging copy — old plugins sometimes need updating first.

Worth fixing

  • WordPress 6.6.2 is behind the current release

    The current release is 6.9. Updating is usually quick, but test it on a copy first if the site has custom code.

    What to doUpdate from the dashboard, ideally on a staging copy first, and keep automatic minor updates on.

  • An old version of jQuery is loaded

    Version 3.4.1 is on the page; 3.7 is current. Old versions have known weaknesses that automated scanners look for.

    What to doUpdate jQuery, or the theme or plugin that bundles it. Check the site afterwards: old code sometimes depends on old behaviour.

Small things

  • Your WordPress version is visible to anyone

    The page announces version 6.6.2 in its source, which tells an attacker exactly which weaknesses to try.

    What to doRemove the generator tag and the ?ver= numbers on your files — most security plugins have a switch for both.

  • Your server announces its PHP version

    Version 8.1.27 appears in the response headers, which gives an attacker one more clue about what to try.

    What we sawPHP/8.1.27

    What to doSet expose_php = Off in your PHP settings, or strip the X-Powered-By header at the server.

Being found on Google

The technical side of search: whether search engines can read the page, list it, and tell it apart from its own duplicates.

Worth fixing

  • There's no description for search results

    The description is the grey text under your link in Google. Writing it yourself is free, and it decides whether people click.

    What to doAdd a meta description of about 150 characters, written for a person rather than for a search engine.

  • The canonical address points somewhere else

    The page says the real version of itself lives at another address. When that is a mistake — a copied template, a staging address left behind, a plugin pointing everything at the home page — search engines drop this page and list the other one instead.

    What we sawhttps://boulangerie-exemple.ca/home/

    What to doCheck the canonical address is the page's own. If it isn't deliberate, point it back at this page.

  • Your site answers at two different addresses

    Both the www and the plain version load on their own instead of one sending visitors to the other. Search engines can treat them as two sites and split what each has earned.

    What we sawhttps://www.boulangerie-exemple.ca/

    What to doPick one, and permanently redirect the other to it. Then set the same one in Search Console.

  • Missing pages don't say they're missing

    We asked for an address that cannot exist and the site answered as though it were a real page. Search engines then index those empty pages, and visitors following a dead link have no idea they hit one.

    What we sawhttps://boulangerie-exemple.ca/maw-site-check-does-not-exist-8f21c4/

    What to doMake missing addresses return a 404 status with a page that says so and offers a way back.

  • No sitemap found

    A sitemap lists your pages for search engines. Without it, newer pages can take much longer to appear.

    What to doPublish a sitemap at /sitemap.xml, name it in robots.txt, and submit it in Google Search Console.

  • The page doesn't say which character set it uses

    Without it a browser has to guess, and accents are what break first: Montréal turns into MontrÃ©al on someone's screen.

    What to doAdd <meta charset="utf-8"> as the first line inside the page head.

  • There's very little text on the page (25 words)

    Search engines match people's questions against words on the page. A page carried by images, sliders or video has little for them to match, and little for a visitor skimming to decide.

    What to doWrite a few hundred words that answer what someone would actually ask before hiring you.

Small things

  • The page title is long (95 characters)

    Google cuts titles off around 60 characters, so the end of yours may never be read.

    What we sawHome | Boulangerie Exemple | Fresh bread, pastries, coffee and catering in the Quebec City area

    What to doTrim it to about 60 characters and put the words people actually search for first.

  • The headings skip a level

    Headings are meant to nest like a table of contents. Skipping a level makes the structure harder to follow for screen readers and for search engines working out what belongs to what.

    What we sawh1 → h3

    What to doUse heading levels in order (h1, then h2, then h3) and choose the size with styling rather than the level.

  • Your address goes through 2 redirects

    Each hop costs time before anything appears, and a little of the value that links pass on. Chains usually build up over the years: http to https, then www, then a new address.

    What we sawhttps://boulangerie-exemple.ca → https://boulangerie-exemple.ca/ → https://boulangerie-exemple.ca/

    What to doRedirect straight from the first address to the last one, in a single step.

  • Links to your site share badly

    There's no preview image or title set, so posting your link on Facebook, LinkedIn or in a text message shows a bare address.

    What to doAdd og:title, og:description and an og:image of about 1200 × 630 pixels.

  • No structured details for search engines

    A small block of hidden data can put your address, hours, prices or reviews directly in search results.

    What to doAdd JSON-LD for your business: LocalBusiness with your address and hours is the usual starting point.

  • Your robots.txt doesn't mention a sitemap

    It is the one place every crawler looks for the list of your pages, and it costs one line.

    What to doAdd a line reading Sitemap: followed by the full address of your sitemap.

  • The page links to very little of your own site (1 links)

    Links between your own pages are how search engines find the rest of the site and work out which pages matter. They are also how a visitor gets anywhere from here.

    What to doLink from this page to your main services and contact page, using words that say where the link goes.

English and French

What the law asks of a business in Quebec, and whether search engines understand which version is which.

Deal with these first

  • We found no French version

    If you do business in Quebec, your site has to be available in French, at least as prominently as any other language. That's the Charter of the French Language, updated by Bill 96, not a preference.

    What to doHave the site translated into French and published at its own addresses, then link the two versions to each other.

On a phone

Most people arrive on a phone. This is whether the page was built for that.

Worth fixing

  • Visitors can't zoom in

    The page blocks pinch-to-zoom. Anyone who needs bigger text — which is a lot of people over forty — simply can't get it, and it fails accessibility rules for that reason.

    What we sawwidth=device-width, initial-scale=1, user-scalable=no

    What to doRemove user-scalable=no and maximum-scale from the viewport tag.

Speed

How long someone waits before they see anything, and what is making them wait.

Worth fixing

  • The server took 2800 ms to answer

    That's before anything is drawn on screen. Slow answers usually mean cheap hosting, no caching, or a plugin doing too much work on every visit.

    What to doTurn on page caching first, then look at the hosting plan. Both are cheaper than a rebuild.

  • The page isn't compressed

    Text compresses to roughly a quarter of its size, and every browser has supported it for twenty years. Sending 234 KB uncompressed is time your visitor waits for nothing.

    What to doTurn on gzip or brotli compression at the server, or ask your host to.

  • 6 scripts hold up the first paint

    They have to finish loading before anything appears on screen, so the visitor watches a blank page while they do.

    What we saw/wp-includes/js/jquery/jquery-3.4.1.min.js

    What to doMark them defer or async, or load them after the page has drawn.

Small things

  • The page's own code is on the large side (234 KB)

    Not a problem yet, but worth watching as you add to the site.

    What to doKeep an eye on it as pages grow; removing an unused plugin usually takes the most off.

  • 7 separate stylesheets load before the page appears

    Each one is a separate trip to the server, and the page can't be drawn until the last one arrives. It usually means several plugins each bringing their own.

    What to doCombine them, or remove the plugins whose styles this page never uses.

  • 6 images have no size set

    The page jumps around as images load, which is what makes people mis-tap buttons and lose their place.

    What to doSet width and height on every image tag, even when the styling resizes it.

  • 6 images use older formats

    JPEG and PNG files are typically a third larger than the same picture in WebP or AVIF, at the same quality. On image-heavy pages that is the single biggest saving available.

    What to doConvert them to WebP with a fallback. Most hosts and image plugins do it automatically.

Everyone can use it

Whether someone using a screen reader, a keyboard or larger text can get through the page.

Worth fixing

  • 6 of 6 images have no text description

    Screen readers announce nothing for these, and search engines can't tell what they show. The description also appears when an image fails to load.

    What to doDescribe what each image shows. Leave alt="" — empty, but present — on purely decorative ones.

Small things

  • One link has nothing to read out

    These are usually icons: a phone, an envelope, a social network. A screen reader announces them as “link”, with no way to know where they go.

    What to doGive each one an aria-label, or alt text on the icon inside it, saying where it leads.

Privacy law

Law 25 in Quebec: what you may collect, and what you have to ask before collecting it.

Worth fixing

  • Tracking appears to run before visitors agree

    We found analytics or advertising scripts and no consent banner. In Quebec, Law 25 requires consent before tracking, with a refusal that is as easy as agreeing.

    What to doHold the tracking scripts until someone accepts, and make declining one click, not a settings hunt.

What we'd suggest
